# Access source catalog

Every source found by the 23 November 2026 research pass, by channel: 319 sources in 14 channels. Read `first-principles-report.md` first; this is the list behind it.

**How to read it.**

- **Value** is 1 to 5: how much real access to top-ranked or private clubs the source adds for an ordinary golfer.
- **Terms** is the verdict on automated access: Allow (no ban, robots.txt open to a generic bot), Deny (a ban or a block), Unclear, or Not checked.
- **Route** is the best lawful way for the site to get the listings.
- **Check** says whether a second agent re-opened the source: confirmed, corrected (it exists but a claim was wrong, and the note says what), or not re-checked. Only the ten highest-value sources per channel were re-checked. The last three channels were not re-checked at all.
- A verdict here is research, not a decision. Nothing is switched on by this file. `data/overrides/platforms.yaml` is what the pipeline obeys.

## Donated rounds: recurring golf-round auctions run by golf organizations

A private club's board controls the tee sheet, but two kinds of people can give a slot away: staff with authority to comp a tee time (superintendent, head professional, general manager) and individual members, who may bring guests. This channel turns both into inventory. Staff donate an unaccompanied foursome to their own trade body's cause: superintendents to GCSAA's Rounds 4 Research, professionals to PGA REACH section auctions, clubs to their state golf association's foundation. Members donate a round with themselves as host, through Member For a Day or a junior-golf auction. They do it because the ask comes from a peer, an idle weekday tee time costs the club almost nothing, and charity play sets no precedent for open access. Supply is capped by that goodwill: usually one foursome per club per cause per year, and the highest-ranked clubs mostly appear only as member-hosted lots. Bidding is open to the public; the only rule I saw stated was age 18 or older, with no membership or handicap asked. Cost is the winning bid plus caddie or cart fees: 2026 Rounds 4 Research openers for private foursomes ran $467 to $2,300, while Youth on Course packages averaged over $5,000. Each organization lists once a year in the same week, bidding lasts one to three weeks, and the auction close ends the opportunity.

**Findings.**

- A catalog of links does work for this channel. Supply comes from about 20 organizers that repeat in the same week each year, and each has its own announcement page stating the dates. None of the terms I read today restricts plain links (32auctions is silent on linking). What the terms restrict is how the site learns the link and close date: not by crawling WinningBidder, PledgeIt, Handbid or Member For a Day, but by…
- Rounds 4 Research is the one source to win. It had more than 1,900 rounds in a single week in 2026 and raised $936,298, which works out to roughly $470 a round. One event link is easy; the lot-by-lot catalog needs GCSAA's consent. Chapters keep most of the proceeds, so more bidders is in GCSAA's interest. Ask rounds4research@gcsaa.org for a lot export or permission to index before the April 2027 sale.
- The state-chapter versions are mostly not separate auctions. Rio Grande, the Pacific Northwest chapters and Georgia all point to the national catalog. The only separate, recurring chapter auction I confirmed is Minnesota's (47 items). Model one national event plus a few independents, not a hundred chapter auctions.
- Supply clusters in two seasons: Masters week through late April, and the last week of October through Veterans Day. Build the timeline on recurrence rules (organizer plus usual week) with states such as expected, announced, open, closing soon and closed. That lets the site show expected April 2027 entries now, when nothing is open, and December to March is the time for permission requests.
- Key each record to the organizer, not the bidding URL. New England PGA moved from 32auctions to PledgeIt in 2026, Southern Texas PGA used OneCause in 2023, and the Rounds 4 Research bidding address is a new item-list ID each year. Stable entry points exist: rounds4research.com, nepga.com/how-to-give, carolinasgolf.org/cgf-annual-auction_2026, ncga.org/online-auction.
- 32auctions publishes a sitemap of individual auctions with last-modified dates, and its robots.txt permits those pages. Slugs carry readable names and years. A daily sitemap diff filtered on golf words is a low-cost discovery feed that never opens an item page. Its terms require written consent to show its content, so store a link, dates and the site's own summary only.
- Two close-date traps showed up. Extended bidding moves the real end past the announced one (Minnesota). Hybrid auctions end in the room, and the platform header can show a meaningless later date (NJ Evans Scholars showed Sep 30 for a late-July event). Store the organizer's stated close, flag hybrids, and label lots that end at an event.
- There are two price tiers. Staff-donated, unaccompanied foursomes (Rounds 4 Research, PGA sections, association foundations) open at a few hundred dollars. Member-hosted lots at top-ranked clubs (Youth on Course, AJGA, Member For a Day) run into the thousands. Tagging each listing as unaccompanied, professional-hosted or member-hosted would tell an ordinary golfer more than the club's rank.

## Golf association member play days at private clubs: Northeast, Mid-Atlantic and Southeast

The tee sheet belongs to each private club (its board or owner). The state or regional Allied Golf Association (AGA) does not own access; it borrows it. Clubs are dues-paying members of the AGA and lend the course for one weekday, usually a Monday or Tuesday when the club is closed or slow. They do it for outside-event revenue on a dead day, to show the course to possible members, and as a favor to the body that rates their course and runs their handicaps. The AGA resells the day to its individual members as a benefit that helps sell GHIN handicap memberships. Supply is capped by one day per host per year, a field of roughly 60 to 96 players, and club willingness: the most famous clubs rarely host open one-days and instead take championships and USGA qualifiers with handicap limits. Eligibility is an active Handicap Index issued through a club in that association; the cheapest route is an online eClub at about $45 to $60 a year. A day costs about $50 to $125, usually with cart. Schedules come out between November and March. Registration opens in waves two weeks to three months ahead and closes 7 to 10 days before play, or sooner when the field fills.

**Calendar.** November to December: next year's schedules start to appear. The Carolinas tentative schedule is published in early November and its Ladies Play Days are added in December; the West Virginia VIP Card goes on pre-order November 1; Alabama and Georgia already link 2027 schedules; Florida's Winter Series runs November to March with entries opening September 1 and October 1. January to March: Southeast play starts (Carolinas senior four-balls from mid January, Virginia from early March, Florida all year) and the Northeast opens registration on fixed days: New Hampshire February 2, Louisiana championships February 1, Maine first Monday of March, Maryland March 3, New York State wave 1 and West Virginia March 4, Met Golf Association March 17 and April 1. April to October: the Northeast season (Connecticut April 11 to October 17, Maine April 24 to October 23, New York State May 4 to September 21, Met area April 29 to October 29, Maryland May 18 to October 19), with second waves on June 1 and 3 and August 1. October to December: Virginia runs to November 16 and the Carolinas to December 10. The date that ends each opportunity is the entry close date: 7 days before play in Virginia and Louisiana, midnight 10 days before in Maine, about 6 days before in Connecticut, about 10 to 12 days before in Florida, and 'when full or 11:59 pm on the close date' in the Carolinas. Only Virginia, Louisiana and Florida print that date on their own pages; elsewhere it must be estimated from the rule or left as the event date. As of November 23, 2026 the channel has almost no live inventory: 2026 seasons are over and no Northeast or Virginia 2027 schedule is posted yet.

## Golf association member play days at private clubs: Midwest, Plains, Mountain and West

The access belongs to each private club: its board, general manager and head professional decide whether to hand a closed Monday or a block of tee times to the state or regional golf association it already belongs to for handicaps and course rating. Clubs appear to say yes for goodwill with the association, a per-player fee on a day the course would otherwise sit empty, and exposure to prospective members (my inference; no page states the motive). The association wants the days because they give public-course and online (eClub) members a reason to pay dues. Supply is limited by the number of willing clubs, roughly one day per club per year, and one tee sheet per day; clubs with waiting lists have little reason to take part, so top-ranked clubs are rare. In the Midwest, Plains, Mountain and West, few associations run a named play-day program. Most of the supply is hidden inside one-day senior, net and partner series that private clubs host. Eligibility is an active association membership with a GHIN number ($38 to $70 a year), sometimes plus age 50 or 60 and a series fee. A round costs $60 to $120 with cart, range and often lunch. Schedules post January to March, registration opens February to mid-March (sometimes by lottery), and each event closes about a week before play or when it fills.

## Competition entry as access: qualifiers, state championships, pro-ams and amateur tours

Access in this channel is controlled by the host club's board, which lends its course for a day (usually a Monday or Tuesday) to a governing body: the USGA acting through its state and regional Allied Golf Associations, the association itself for state championships and one-day series, or a PGA Section or Tour event for pro-ams. Clubs do it for standing in the game, as a duty of association membership, to audition for bigger championships and, for pro-ams, to raise charity or purse money. The outsider never deals with the club; the governing body sells the entry. Supply is capped at about one date per club per year and a field of roughly 48 sides to 84 players per site, and popular sites fill before the deadline. Eligibility is a Handicap Index ceiling plus a GHIN number: 0.4 for the U.S. Open and U.S. Amateur, 2.4 for the Mid-Amateur and Four-Ball, 5.4 for the Senior Amateur, but 9.4 to 14.4 for the women's mid-amateur, senior and four-ball events. State one-day gross and net series take any handicap but require association membership. Cost is low: $85 to $200 for association events and USGA qualifying, against $1,500 to $21,000 for a charity or Tour pro-am spot. Schedules appear November to February, entries open February to April, and each opportunity ends at a published entry-close date one to ten weeks before play.

**Calendar.** The end date for almost everything here is an entry-close date set by the governing body, not the day of play. ## Paid access networks, societies and memberships

A private club's tee sheet belongs to its members, acting through a board or an owner-operator. Outsiders get on only when someone with authority chooses to sell or lend a slot. In this channel three parties do that. (1) Management companies (Invited, Troon, TPC, Heritage, Dormie, McConnell) let members of one club play the others. It costs them little and helps sell memberships, so the key is a membership, not a ticket. (2) Clubs rent the whole course on a closed day to a society or media brand (The Golfer's Journal, Fried Egg, The Outpost Club), which resells about 100 seats. The club earns outing revenue without displacing members. (3) Operators with spare off-peak capacity sell capped rounds through a sponsor: Visa Infinite at Troon Privé clubs, PGA Section summer passes. Supply is limited by closed days per club, field size, per-club caps (two rounds a year on the Visa program, three on Private Club Network) and members' tolerance for strangers. Most programs require an existing private-club membership. A golfer without one can buy in through society dues ($90 to $275 a year plus $1,000 or more per event), a card, a $65 pass, or an invitation-only society with five-figure fees. Dated opportunities are announced each autumn for the next year and close on a lottery window or a sell-out. Network and card benefits run year-round with no end date.

## Golf media, community and architecture-society events at private clubs

The access belongs to the host club. Its board or general manager agrees to sell a closed day (usually a Monday or a shoulder-season date) to a media brand or society it trusts, and that organizer then controls the guest list. Clubs do it for buyout revenue on a day members aren't playing, for friendly coverage from architecture-minded media, and because the organizer vouches for the guests. Organizers do it because events are what make a $90 to $275 subscription worth paying for. Architect societies get in because clubs like honoring their designer. Supply is capped at about one day per club per year and fields of roughly 34 to 180 players, so demand far exceeds spots. Organizers ration by lottery (The Golfer's Journal) or by a published on-sale time with a 24 to 48 hour head start for paying members (Fried Egg, No Laying Up). Anyone can buy those memberships. A few circles (Outpost Club, Robert Trent Jones Society) are closed to the public. Costs seen today: $200 for a local Roost major, $375 at a public course, $875 to $1,900 for a private-club day, and $2,850 to $9,500 for trips, plus the membership. Calendars for the next year appear October through December. Each event then opens three to six months ahead. The date that ends the opportunity is the lottery close or the on-sale moment, not the event date.

## Public, resort and hotel-gated access to top-ranked courses

Access in this channel is controlled by the course owner, not by members: resort companies (Pebble Beach, Pinehurst, Dream Golf, Kohler, the PGA TOUR's TPC network, MGM), governments (New York State at Bethpage, Pierce County at Chambers Bay) and a small set of private clubs that own lodging. They let outsiders in because outsiders are the business. A tee time sells rooms, caddies and meals, so the best inventory is tied to a stay and released first to overnight guests. Governments ration by residency and short booking windows instead. Private clubs with beds (Sea Island, Bay Hill, Firestone, Dismal River, The Prairie Club, Watersound through Camp Creek Inn) sell spare tee times and rooms, usually with a cap such as one public trip a year or Sunday-to-Thursday play. Supply is limited by daylight tee times and room count, not by member goodwill. Eligibility is mostly money: anyone who pays, sometimes with a required stay; no sponsor and usually no handicap check. **Findings.**

- This is the most open channel and a catalog of links fits it well. Almost everything is for sale to anyone, so the scarce thing is knowing the rule and the window. One hand-written access record per course (who can play, price, window, link to the operator's own policy page) covers it, and the facts change about once a year. No crawl is needed, which removes most of the terms problem.
- An end-date-only timeline will miss most of this channel. Of roughly 25 operators checked, only a handful have a real end date (The Prairie Club's Draw on 7 Dec 2026, association trip deadlines). The rest need two more fields: an opens-on date (Chambers Bay 1 Jan 2027, The Prairie Club 11 Jan 2027) and a rolling window in days before play (Bethpage 5 or 7, TPC Sawgrass 14, Streamsong 30, Kiawah 60, Chambers Bay 90).
- Linking is not always free. TPC's terms (Section 6(F)) forbid any link without written permission except a text-only link to the home page, so TPC Sawgrass needs a permission request or a home-page link. GOLF's terms allow a plain-text link but no framing; Top100GolfCourses permits ordinary links to pages.
- Tee-time marketplaces will not deliver the top inventory. The marquee resorts link to their own booking engines: Agilysys or rGuest at Pinehurst, Kiawah, Sand Valley and Streamsong, foreUP at Bethpage, EZLinks at Chambers Bay and TPC, TeeItUp at Pasatiempo. A GolfNow or Supreme Golf feed would add ordinary public tee times, not top-ranked or private access.
- GolfNow and TeeOff both advertise API integrations and website monetization by application form, with no published terms or named network. That route would add affiliate income, which conflicts with the project's ads-only rule and with the rule on adding data sources, so it is the owner's decision.

## The clubs own doors: guest policies, reciprocal requests, outside outings and off-season play

Each private club controls its own door. The board and members set policy; the head professional and general manager apply it. There is no central registry, and most clubs publish only etiquette rules for guests who already have a member host. Clubs let outsiders in for five reasons: a member vouches for them; professional courtesy between PGA professionals (your home pro asks their pro); money on idle days (Monday outings, shoulder season, event weeks such as the Masters); recruiting (new clubs sell preview rounds and discovery visits to find members); and charity or community standing. Supply is limited by tee-sheet capacity, by members' dislike of outside play, and, from memory and not checked today, by tax rules that cap non-member income at member-owned clubs. Eligibility is the real gate: most doors need a member or a home-club professional, so a golfer with no club has few options. The doors open to anyone who pays are rare and mostly at new clubs, Augusta-area clubs in Masters week, and clubs tied to a hotel. Prices seen today run from $249 to $299 for a club-run guest day in Hawaii to $250 per player for a preview round; accompanied guest fees are almost never published. Standing policies have no end date. Dated windows have a booking-open date, a play window and a sell-out.

**Calendar.** Most of this channel is standing policy with no end date, so it belongs in a per-club directory, not on the timeline. The dated part is small and follows this calendar. - October 1: Old Barnwell opens booking for its April window and for preview play (stated on its page for the 2026-27 cycle). End date to use: sell-out or last play date for event-week and preview windows; the stated request-through date for request windows; none for reciprocal, hotel-key and discovery doors. A booking-opens date matters as much as the end date here, because the Masters-week inventory sold out about five months ahead.

## Outing and tournament platforms, directories and public-record discovery not yet checked

The club's board and general manager own the tee sheet. Outsiders get in when the club sells a closed-course block, usually a Monday when the course would otherwise sit idle, to an organizer: a charity, hospital foundation, chamber, trade association, amateur tour, or a retail operator such as Golf Moose. The club does it for outing and food revenue on a dead day, to back members' causes, and to show the club to prospects. The organizer, not the club, sells the spots and publishes the page, which is why listings are scattered across thousands of one-off sites and registration tools. Supply is capped by Mondays in season, each club's limit on outside events, and a field of roughly 72 to 144 players. The highest-ranked clubs host few such days and fill them through sponsors before any public page exists, so this channel reaches mostly mid-tier private clubs. Eligibility is simply paying. Trade groups publish a slightly higher non-member price; amateur tours add a tour membership and a handicap flight. Prices seen today: $95 to $169 for retail Monday events in California, $175 to $525 per golfer for chamber and association outings, $300 and up for hospital and charity events, often foursome-only. Clubs book 6 to 12 months ahead. Registration opens two to five months out and ends at sell-out or one to two weeks before play, when the roster goes to the club. That registration cutoff, not the play date, is the end date.

**Calendar.** Yearly shape of this channel: - November to January: clubs and organizers fix next season's dates (Invited recommends booking 6 to 12 months ahead). Amateur tour memberships open (Golfweek tour: Nov 27 2026 for 2027). California retail private-club Mondays run through winter; the schedule is posted about four months ahead. - February to April: chamber, association and charity registration pages go live for May to October play; early-bird prices start. - May to October: peak outing season in most of the country, almost all on Mondays. Early-bird cutoffs fall four to eight weeks before play, final cutoffs one to two weeks before. - Year-round: IRS posts 990 batches monthly; a given year's golf events surface 6 to 24 months later, so that data updates the charity list each winter, not the timeline. Which date ends each opportunity: 1. The stated registration or entry deadline when the page gives one (Greenskeeper: exactly 7 days before play; Kenosha Chamber: Aug 14 for Aug 24). 2. For Golf Moose pages, the refund and pairing cutoff two weeks before play, since after that the roster goes to the club. 3. Sell-out, which often comes first (ACG NJ sold out; Sioux Falls chamber sold out). Show a sold-out state and keep the listing for next year's cycle. 4. If no deadline is stated, default to play date minus 7 days and label it as estimated. Also store the early-bird date as a secondary marker; it is the date most pages actually print.

**Findings.**

- On the catalog-of-links question: for most platforms marked DENY, the clause bans automated access, not links. Exceptions found: BlueGolf's terms restrict deep links themselves, and SchoolAuction.net and Unknown Golf also bar manual monitoring or copying without consent. The hard part is discovery, which still…
- Golf Moose is the highest-value new source: single spots at private California clubs for $95 to $169, open to anyone, 16 events in five weeks. Its terms ban bots, so the route is a permission or referral request to jeremy@golfmoose.com. Greenskeeper.org mirrors the schedule on one page with exact entry deadlines.
- Google shut its standalone events search around September 2026 (DataForSEO withdrew its Events endpoint on Sept 15) and the Custom Search JSON API is closed to new customers and ends Jan 1 2027. Event markup no longer has a Google-run query surface, so do not design around an events API. Use ordinary organic queries through the existing SERP provider.
- Do not trust schema.org Event markup from registration platforms without checking it. Two different Perfect Golf Event pages returned the same generic event name, the same date and a placeholder venue. At web scale, Event markup sits on about 399,000 hosts (Oct 2024 crawl), but the golf share is unknown.
- ChamberMaster/GrowthZone sites expose a uniform RSS feed (/Feed/rss/UpcomingEvents.rss) and SiteMap.xml, robots allows /events/, and GrowthZone's terms bind the chamber, not visitors. One parser could cover thousands of chambers and builders associations; filter titles for golf. Verified on one chamber so far.
- Platforms split into those with a public index (Perfect Golf Event: about 16,000 to 17,000 event subdomains in one sitemap) and those with none (Event Caddy, Charity Golf Today, DoJiggy, the auction tools). For the second group only search results or organizer submissions can find events, whatever the terms say.
- Form 990 Schedule G can produce a national list of charities that run golf events (more than 10,000 filings mention golf tournament), with EIN, state and usually a website. It cannot give dates or venues and misses any charity whose golf event is not one of its two largest. Best use: seed targeted searches and an organizer-submission outreach list each winter.
- State raffle and auction licensing is a dead end where I could read it: California registers raffles per organization per year with one aggregate report, and silent auctions need no registration at all.

## Sanctioned routes into auction and package platforms: APIs, affiliate feeds, partner programs

Auction platforms own none of this access; they are plumbing. The access belongs to the club (its board sets how many guest rounds it donates) and to individual members (who donate their own hosted guest slots). They release it for three reasons: to back a charity a member cares about at almost no cost to the club, to fund golf's own causes (superintendent research, PGA section and state association foundations, junior golf), and, at resorts, to fill slow dates and earn a cut (TravelPledge's provider side pays courses up to 40% of retail value). Supply is capped by club guest policy, so it arrives as a few foursomes per club per auction, sometimes with a member host required and caddie fees extra. Anyone who registers can bid; no handicap or membership check appeared in any terms I read. Cost is the winning bid: 2026 Rounds 4 Research opening bids ran about $470 to $2,300 per foursome at well-known private clubs; Member For a Day keeps 22% and adds roughly 5.5% processing. Consignment suppliers (Winspire, Mitch-Stuart, AmFund) are different: resort trips anyone could book, sold only through nonprofits at a markup. Listings appear days to weeks before close; one-week golf-industry auctions recur each spring and November; every lot ends at its auction close.

## Existing aggregators, guides and golfer demand

Three parties hold the keys. The club's board decides whether the course is rented to an outside group for a day. Individual members can host guests within guest limits and can donate a hosted round to a charity. The superintendents' association collects donated rounds from clubs each spring (Rounds 4 Research). They let outsiders in for four reasons: outing revenue, charity, a member's favor, and membership leads (the stated reason private clubs give GolfNow a few tee times). Supply is capped by the tee sheet, guest rules and the club's wish to stay private, and nobody publishes an inventory feed. So existing players split into two camps. Member-to-member networks (Thousand Greens, OpenRounds, Outpost Club, Eighty Club) are closed to non-members by design. The public options are one outing directory (Play Private Golf), one auction house (Member For a Day), paid society calendars (Golfer's Journal, Fried Egg, Troon Access) and aging magazine guides. On the open routes, eligibility is mostly ability to pay: about $300 to $600 for an outing seat, a few hundred to several thousand dollars for an auctioned foursome, $1,000 for a Troon day, plus $120 to $275 in yearly dues for society events. Outings are posted months ahead. The big auctions run four to nine days in the same weeks each year. Society events open registration on a stated date and fill.

## Work-for-play: tournament volunteering, association course-rating crews and club work that comes with a round

Two gatekeepers control this channel, and neither is the club. (1) Tournament organizers (a local host organization or an agency such as Outlyr, Octagon or Pro Links Sports, the PGA of America, the USGA) need roughly 600 to 2,000+ unpaid workers for one week. The club only lends its course. A round afterward is a retention perk the club has to agree to, so it shows up mainly at smaller Korn Ferry and Champions stops that have to work to fill shifts, and almost never at majors or the best-known clubs, where the credential alone fills the roster. Supply is capped by the tee-sheet days a club will give up: one play day, or vouchers inside a fixed window. Anyone can sign up (usually 18+, juniors from 13 or 14 without golf). Cost is a $0 to $245 uniform fee plus 2 to 5 shifts (12 to 30 hours). Registration opens 4 to 10 months ahead and closes when committees fill; few pages print a close date. (2) State and regional golf associations control course-rating teams. Member clubs are re-rated about every ten years and playing the course is part of the procedure, so raters get onto private clubs as the job itself. Supply is tiny: 20 to 50 ratings a year per association, trained teams, several waiting lists. (3) Club jobs and caddie programs are employment, not listings.

**Calendar.** Two dates matter per item, and they are different: (a) registration close, which ends the chance to sign up, and (b) the redemption window, which ends the chance to play. Most pages print neither a firm close date nor a fill status, so the timeline needs a fallback: "opens" date, then "open until committees fill", with the tournament's first day as the hard stop. ## Schools as gatekeepers: university-owned ranked courses, alumni and booster eligibility, and college-am fundraisers

Universities control this access three ways: they own courses (run by athletics or an auxiliary), their teams are guests of private clubs for invitationals, and their alumni and booster groups rent private clubs for Monday outings. They let outsiders in for money and goodwill. Green fees pay for a course the team needs; a college-am or outing funds the golf program or scholarships. Supply is limited by the tee sheet left after team, student and member play, by policy (some courses stay affiliate-only), by one college-am day per tournament with roughly 20 to 32 groups, and by each club's quota of outside events. Eligibility is a ladder: public; alumni or staff rate; degree required (Michigan); donor or member (Kampen-Cosler, Jimmie Austin, UT Golf Club). Where the public may play, the key is a green fee of $45 to $350. Where a key must be bought, it is a booster gift or a national membership of about $1,500 to $5,000 a year. A degree cannot be bought, so the only route left at degree-gated courses is as a graduate's guest. Course access is a standing rule with rolling booking windows of 5 to 90 days. Only passes, college-ams and outings carry dates; they are announced one to four months ahead and end at a registration deadline or a full field.

**Calendar.** Yearly shape of this channel, with the date that ends each item. - November to February: membership and season-pass sales for the coming year. Purdue's 2027 memberships and Seven Oaks' 2027 early registration are on sale now; neither page posts a deadline. Radrick Farms posts its yearly guide in late January. End date: the early-bird deadline where one is stated, otherwise none. - Late February to April: spring college invitationals. ## Entry by ticket, pledge or points: raffles, sweepstakes, fundraise-to-play golf marathons and loyalty-point auctions

A round at a private club belongs to the club and its members, and none of it is sold directly. In this channel a club or a member gives a tee time or a closed-course day to a cause, and the cause turns it into money in one of three ways: an entry fee plus a fundraising minimum (golf marathons, the Els for Autism regionals), a chance (raffle tickets), or a marketing prize (tournament and brand sweepstakes). Loyalty programs are a fourth holder: they buy hospitality and resell it for points. Clubs agree because the date is a Monday or shoulder-season day, the cause belongs to a member or to golf itself (Youth on Course, a PGA Section, a hall of fame), and the guest list stays small. Supply is capped by how many closed days a board will give up and by state raffle law, which licenses each drawing and often limits who can buy. Eligibility runs from anyone who registers and fundraises, to in-state buyers for many raffles, to US residents 18 and older for sweepstakes, to program members holding points. Cost runs from free (sweepstakes), to $20-$100 a ticket, to $1,000-$2,500 pledged, to 300,000-plus points. Dates are set weeks to months ahead: registration close or event day for marathons, ticket cutoff and drawing for raffles, entry-period end for sweepstakes, auction close for points.
