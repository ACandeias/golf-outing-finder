A charity golf outing can raise real money for a cause, bring a community together and become an event people look forward to every year. It also takes more planning than most first-time organizers expect. The good news is that the work follows a predictable pattern, and once you've done it once, the second year is much easier.

This guide walks through the main pieces: when to start, how to pick a course, how to build a budget, how to find sponsors, how to handle registration, and how to staff the day.

## Start with a goal

Before you call a course, decide what success looks like. A few questions help:

- How much do you want to raise after costs?
- How many golfers can you realistically attract? A full shotgun field on an 18-hole course is often around 144 players, which is two foursomes on each hole, but many good outings run with fewer.
- Who's coming? Supporters of your cause, local businesses, a school community, a company's clients?
- What kind of day do you want? A relaxed morning scramble with lunch, or an afternoon round followed by a dinner and auction?

Your answers shape everything else, from the course you choose to the price you charge.

## A rough timeline

Every event is different, but this is a reasonable schedule for a first outing.

### Nine to twelve months out

- Form a small committee and pick a date range.
- Contact courses and book one. Popular dates in peak months go early.
- Set a rough budget and a fundraising goal.

### Six to eight months out

- Build your sponsor packet and start asking businesses.
- Set up your registration page and publish your event page.
- Line up prizes, contest holes and any hole-in-one insurance.

### Two to four months out

- Push registration through your network, email and social posts.
- Confirm food, carts and the schedule with the course.
- Order sponsor signs once artwork is in.

### The last month

- Assign teams and starting holes.
- Recruit and brief volunteers.
- Confirm final numbers with the course, which usually wants a headcount a week or two ahead.

## Picking a course

Outings happen at every kind of course, and each has its own trade-offs.

- **Municipal and public courses** often have lower per-player costs and are used to hosting community events. Our guide to [outings at municipal courses](/guides/outings-at-municipal-courses) has more on what to expect.
- **Semi-private courses** can offer a middle ground, with member-level conditions and some flexibility for outside events.
- **Private clubs** often host outings on days the club is closed to members, frequently Mondays. They may need a member to sponsor the event, and costs tend to be higher. Ask early how the club handles outside events.
- **Resorts** can work well for destination events or multi-day gatherings, but costs and minimums vary widely.

When you call, ask about the per-player price and what it includes, minimum player counts, food and beverage minimums, cart availability, rain policy, deposit and cancellation terms, and whether the course allows outside sponsors' signs and tables.

## Building a budget

Most outing budgets have two sides.

**Costs** usually include the course fee per player (green fee, cart and range), food and drinks, prizes and awards, gift bag items, sponsor signs, printing, hole-in-one insurance if you offer a big prize, registration platform fees and payment processing, and any permits you need.

**Income** comes from entry fees, sponsorships, mulligans and contests, raffles and auctions, and direct donations.

A common approach is to set the entry fee so it covers your per-player costs with a margin, then rely on sponsorships and extras for most of the money raised. Check what similar outings near you charge so your price feels fair for the course and what's included.

## Finding sponsors

Sponsors are often the biggest source of money raised. Build a simple packet with a few clear levels: hole sponsors, contest sponsors, meal or cart sponsors, foursome packages and one presenting sponsor. Each level should list its price, what it includes and the deadline for artwork.

Start with people who already know your cause: board members, past donors, local businesses your supporters use. A personal ask from someone they know works far better than a cold email. Our guide to [sponsoring a hole](/guides/sponsoring-a-hole) explains what businesses typically get, which helps you price the levels.

## Registration

You can take registrations in a few ways. Golf-specific registration platforms handle teams, sponsors, contests and scoring. General fundraising and ticketing platforms are simpler and may already be in use by your organization. Some groups use a simple form on their own website.

Whatever you choose, compare fees on both the organizer's side and the player's side, and check whether golfers can sign up as a single, as a pair or as a full foursome. Accepting singles helps fill the field.

Your public event page matters too. Put the date, course name and city, shotgun time, price per player and per foursome, what's included and the registration link in plain text on the page. That makes it easier for golfers to decide, and easier for directories like ours to list you accurately.

## Contests, raffles and extras

Contests and raffles add fun and money. Closest to the pin, longest drive and a putting contest are easy to run. Raffles and 50/50s are popular but regulated, and rules differ by state, so check yours before selling tickets. Our guide to [mulligans, raffles and contests](/guides/mulligans-raffles-and-contests) covers the options.

## Volunteers

A smooth outing needs people. Typical roles include registration and check-in, contest holes, raffle and auction tables, sponsor greeting, photos and cleanup. Give each volunteer a short written brief with times and a contact person. Thank them during the awards.

## The day itself

Arrive early, set up registration well before players arrive, and walk the course to place signs. Hold a short welcome before the shotgun start that covers the format, the contests and the weather policy. Keep the program after the round brief. People appreciate a quick awards session and a sincere thank-you to sponsors.

## After the event

Send thank-you notes to sponsors and players within a week, share how much was raised, and send any required donation receipts. Then book next year's date while the course still has the calendar open.
