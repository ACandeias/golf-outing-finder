If you pay $250 to play in a charity golf outing, can you deduct $250 on your taxes? Usually not all of it. Part of what you pay is a gift to the charity, and part pays for the golf, cart, food and gifts you get in return. This guide explains the general federal rules, based on IRS guidance including [Publication 1771](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p1771.pdf), so you know what questions to ask.

**This is general information, not tax advice.** Tax rules change, your situation is your own, and state rules may differ. Talk to a tax professional before you claim a deduction.

## The key idea: you can't deduct what you got back

The IRS calls a payment that's partly a gift and partly a purchase a "quid pro quo contribution." When you pay a charity and receive goods or services in return, the amount you can treat as a charitable contribution is generally limited to what you paid above the fair market value of what you received.

At a golf outing, what you receive usually includes:

- the round of golf (the green fee)
- a cart
- range balls
- meals and drinks
- a gift bag or shirt
- anything else included in the entry

### An illustrative example

Say you pay $250 to enter a charity scramble. The organizer estimates that the round, cart, lunch and dinner you receive are worth $140, roughly what they'd cost if you bought them yourself at that course. Under the general rule, the most you could treat as a contribution would be $110 ($250 minus $140).

The numbers here are made up for illustration. The real value depends on the course, the meals and what's included, which is why the organizer's estimate matters.

### If you don't play

IRS Publication 526 says that whether you use a ticket or privilege to a charity benefit event doesn't change the amount you can deduct. If you paid and then didn't show up, the value of what you were entitled to still counts against the deduction. The publication notes one exception: if you return the ticket to the organization so it can resell it, you can deduct the full amount you paid. Ask the organizer if you can't attend.

## What the charity should give you

Publication 1771 describes two written documents that matter for outings.

### A disclosure statement for payments over $75

When a charity receives a quid pro quo payment of more than $75, it must give you a written disclosure statement. The statement must:

- tell you that the deductible amount is limited to what you paid above the value of the goods or services you received, and
- give a good-faith estimate of the fair market value of those goods or services.

The charity can provide this when it asks for the payment or when it receives it. In practice, you might see it on the registration page, in a confirmation email or on a receipt. For example, an outing page might say something like "Of the $250 entry fee, $140 is the estimated value of golf and meals." If you can't find one, ask the organizer.

The IRS can penalize a charity that doesn't provide the required disclosure: $10 per contribution, up to $5,000 per fundraising event or mailing, according to Publication 1771.

### A written acknowledgment for contributions of $250 or more

For any single contribution of $250 or more, you need a contemporaneous written acknowledgment from the charity before you can claim the deduction. Publication 1771 says it should include the charity's name, the amount of any cash contribution, whether the charity provided goods or services in return, and if it did, a description and good-faith estimate of their value.

"Contemporaneous" means you need it by the date you file your return for that year, or the return's due date including extensions, whichever comes first. Many charities send these by the end of January.

### Records for any cash gift

For any monetary contribution, the IRS requires a record such as a bank or credit card statement, a canceled check or a written communication from the charity. Keep your registration confirmation and payment record together.

## Is the organizer a qualified charity?

Only gifts to qualified organizations can be deducted. Many outings are run by 501(c)(3) charities, but others are run by groups that aren't, such as some booster clubs, social clubs or fundraisers for an individual family. A good cause isn't automatically a qualified charity.

You can check an organization with the IRS [Tax Exempt Organization Search](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/tax-exempt-organization-search). Search by the legal name or EIN. Some eligible organizations, such as churches and government units, may not appear in the search, so ask the organizer if you can't find them.

On Golf Outing Finder, a listing shows a "Charity" label when we've checked the organizer against IRS records. When we couldn't confirm it, the listing says "Fundraiser, charity status unverified." Our label is a convenience, not a determination of whether your payment is deductible. Check the organization yourself and keep the charity's paperwork.

## Entry fees versus sponsorships

Hole sponsorships, cart sponsorships and title sponsorships work differently from entry fees.

- **An individual sponsor** who gets nothing back except a thank-you may be making a larger charitable contribution. If the sponsorship includes a foursome, meals or other benefits, their value generally comes off the top, just as with an entry fee.
- **A business sponsor** that gets its name on a sign, in a program or on a banner is often in a different situation. Depending on what the business receives, the payment may be treated as an advertising or business expense rather than a charitable contribution, with different rules.

If you're sponsoring through your business, this is especially a question for your accountant. Our guide to [sponsoring a hole](/guides/sponsoring-a-hole) covers how sponsorships work at outings.

## Raffles, mulligans and auctions

Extras bought during the day follow their own rules.

- **Raffle tickets.** IRS Publication 526 says you can't deduct amounts you pay for raffle or lottery tickets, bingo or other games of chance.
- **Auction items.** If you buy an item at a charity auction, the general rule is the same: only what you paid above its fair market value may count.
- **Mulligans and on-course games.** You're paying for something during the event. Whether any part counts is a question for your tax professional.

## Whether you can use the deduction at all

Even when part of your fee is a qualified contribution, whether it lowers your tax depends on your return. Historically, most people needed to itemize to benefit. According to the IRS ([Topic 506](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc506)), beginning with tax year 2026, people who don't itemize may deduct up to $1,000 of cash contributions to eligible charities ($2,000 for married couples filing jointly). Limits and exceptions apply, and the rules may change again. A tax professional can tell you what applies to you.

## A short checklist

1. Check the organizer in Tax Exempt Organization Search.
2. Look for the charity's statement of what the goods and services are worth.
3. Keep your payment record and registration confirmation.
4. If your contribution is $250 or more, get the charity's written acknowledgment before you file.
5. Don't count raffle tickets.
6. Talk to a tax professional before claiming anything.
