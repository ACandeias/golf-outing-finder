You don't need three friends to play a golf outing. Plenty of outings take single players and put them on a team, and many golfers who go alone come home with new playing partners. This guide covers how to find outings that welcome singles, how pairing works, and how to make the day go well.

## Can you sign up alone?

Usually, yes. Most charity and community outings sell entries per player as well as per foursome. Organizers want full fields, and a few singles help them fill out teams when a foursome drops to three or a sponsor's group comes up short.

Some outings don't take singles. Corporate outings with invitation-only fields, member-guest events at private clubs, and outings where foursomes are sold only through sponsorship packages may require a full team. If a listing shows only a foursome price, or says "Foursomes through sponsorship," check the organizer's page or contact them before assuming you can join alone.

On Golf Outing Finder, the "singles welcome" filter shows outings that list a single-player price. That's a good starting point.

## How organizers pair singles

Pairing methods vary, but they usually look like one of these:

- **Fill-in with other singles.** The organizer puts singles together into new foursomes. If four people sign up alone, they become a team.
- **Fill-in with short teams.** A group of three gets one single added. A twosome might get two.
- **By handicap or skill.** Some organizers ask your handicap or typical score so they can balance teams. If you don't have a handicap, an honest average score is fine.
- **By request.** If you know someone else who signed up alone, ask the organizer to put you together.

You'll usually learn your team at check-in, when you get your starting hole and cart assignment. Some organizers email pairings a day or two ahead.

## What to say when you register

When you sign up, it helps to give the organizer a little information:

- Your typical score or handicap, if you know it.
- Whether you'd like to ride with a particular person.
- Any preference for a morning or afternoon wave, if the outing runs two.
- Anything about walking, mobility or forward tees that the organizer should know.

None of this is required, but it helps the organizer place you well.

## What the day is like

A single's day looks like everyone else's. You check in, collect any gift bag, eat the included meal and head to the range. The difference is that you'll meet your teammates at the cart or the first tee.

Most outings use a scramble, which makes playing with strangers easy. Everyone hits, the team picks the best shot, and you all play from there. Nobody is keeping close track of your personal score, and a bad shot rarely costs the team anything. If you haven't played one before, see [what is a golf scramble](/guides/what-is-a-golf-scramble).

Your teammates will likely know each other. That's normal, and most groups are glad to have a fourth. A quick introduction, a question about how they're connected to the cause, and you're part of the team.

## Tips for a good day

**Arrive early.** Getting there 60 to 90 minutes before the start gives you time to check in, find your cart, warm up and meet your team before the round.

**Be honest about your game.** If you're a 25 handicap, say so. In a scramble, the team needs to know who's likely to hit the fairway and who's the best putter. Nobody minds a higher handicap; people mind surprises.

**Keep pace in mind.** With a shotgun start, every group is on the course at once. Be ready to hit when it's your turn, and pick up your ball once the team has chosen a shot. For more on the format, see [how shotgun starts work](/guides/how-shotgun-starts-work).

**Join in the extras.** Buying a few raffle tickets or a mulligan is a simple way to support the cause and gives you something to talk about with your team.

**Stay for dinner.** The meal after the round is where people relax, prizes are handed out and the organizers share what the day raised. It's also where single players often end up with an invite to next year's team.

**Follow the course's dress code.** Collared shirts and golf shoes are the safe default. Private clubs may have stricter rules, which we cover in [what to wear to an outing at a private club](/guides/what-to-wear-private-club-outing).

## What it costs

Single prices are usually the foursome price divided by four, sometimes a little more. As an illustrative example, an outing might list $150 per player or $560 per foursome. Weekday outings at municipal courses often run $100 to $175 per player, while private club outings often run $250 to $600 or more. Prices vary by region, course and what's included.

If the outing doesn't list a single price, it doesn't always mean singles aren't welcome. Some organizers prefer you call or email. Others list only a foursome price but will happily place you.

## What if you're nervous about your game?

Outings are full of casual golfers. Many people at a charity scramble play only a handful of rounds a year, and the format is designed for that. As long as you can get the ball around the course and keep moving, you'll fit in. You also don't need an official handicap at most outings.

If you're worried about holding the group up, tell your teammates at the start. Most will be happy to have you hit first on short shots and pick up once the team has a ball in a good spot.

## Choosing an outing for your first solo round

A few things make a good first outing as a single:

- **A scramble format.** The most forgiving way to play with strangers.
- **A single price on the listing.** A sign the organizer expects solo entries.
- **A nearby course.** Filter by distance so you're not driving two hours for your first try.
- **Clear details.** Look for the date and shotgun time, what's included and a recent "Last verified" date.
