Golf outings are planned months ahead, and the weather doesn't care. Sooner or later you'll sign up for one and wake up to rain on the forecast. The good news is that most organizers and courses have been through it before, and there's usually a plan.

This guide covers what typically happens when weather threatens an outing: whether it's played, postponed or shortened, what happens with lightning, and how refunds tend to work. Every event sets its own policy, so the organizer's page is always the final word.

## Who decides whether to play

Two parties are usually involved. The course decides whether the course is playable. Heavy rain can flood bunkers, soak fairways and make it unsafe to drive carts off the paths. The organizer decides what to do with the event: play anyway, delay, shorten it or move it.

A little rain rarely stops an outing. Golfers play in drizzle all the time, and many outings go ahead in steady rain with umbrellas and rain gear. What changes the plan is usually one of these:

- the course closes or limits play because of standing water
- the course requires carts to stay on the paths, which slows the round down a lot
- lightning in the area
- severe weather warnings, high winds or extreme heat

## Rain dates

Some outings announce a rain date when they open registration, often a day or a week later at the same course. Others don't, because finding another open date at the course is hard, especially in the busy months. If the event page lists a rain date, put it on your calendar when you sign up.

If there's no rain date and the event is postponed, the organizer will usually work with the course to pick a new one and tell registered players by email. Not everyone can make the new date, which is why refund and credit policies matter.

## Lightning

Lightning is the one weather condition every course takes seriously. When lightning is close, play stops, and players are expected to leave the course or get to shelter right away.

Many courses use a siren or air horn to signal a stoppage and another signal to resume. The exact signals vary, so listen at the pre-round briefing or ask the pro shop. If you hear the signal, stop. Don't finish the hole, and don't wait to see if it passes. A golf course is an open space with metal clubs, and nobody wants to take that chance.

After a lightning stoppage, play often resumes once the storm has moved on and a waiting period has passed. If storms keep coming, the organizer may call the round.

## Shortened rounds

When weather cuts into the day, organizers have a few common options.

### Play nine holes

Some outings switch to nine holes before the start, or stop after nine if storms arrive mid-round. With a shotgun start, every team starts on a different hole, so stopping partway means each team has played a different set of holes. Organizers usually rank teams on the holes everyone completed or use another simple method they explain at the time. Our guide to [how shotgun starts work](/guides/how-shotgun-starts-work) explains why everyone finishes at the same time.

### Skip the contests or awards

When time is short, contests may be dropped or prizes given out by drawing instead.

### Move straight to the meal

If golf can't be played at all, many outings still hold the lunch or dinner, the raffle and the awards. For a charity event, that part of the day is often where much of the money gets raised, so organizers try hard to keep it.

## Refunds and credits

This is where policies vary the most. Before you register, look for a line about weather on the organizer's page. Common approaches include:

- **No refunds for weather.** Many charity outings say this plainly. Course fees, food and prizes are often committed in advance, and the organizer may not get that money back.
- **Credit for the rescheduled date.** If the event moves, your entry carries over.
- **Partial refund.** Some organizers refund part of the fee, such as the golf portion, if no holes are played.
- **Treat it as a donation.** At charity outings, players are sometimes asked whether they'd like their entry fee to become a gift to the cause. Our guide to whether [charity golf entry fees are tax deductible](/guides/are-charity-golf-entry-fees-tax-deductible) explains the general rules. It's not tax advice.

If you're unsure, email the organizer before the day. They'd rather answer a question in advance than sort out a dozen confused players at check-in.

## How to stay informed on the day

- **Check your email the night before and the morning of.** Most organizers send weather updates to registered players.
- **Check the organizer's page and social accounts.** Changes are often posted there first.
- **Call the course if you can't reach the organizer.** The pro shop will know if the course is open, though the organizer decides what happens with the event.
- **Pack for it.** Rain gear, an umbrella, a towel, extra gloves and dry socks can make a wet round a lot more pleasant.
