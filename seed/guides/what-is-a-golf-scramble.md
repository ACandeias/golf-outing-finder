If you've signed up for a golf outing, there's a good chance it's a scramble. It's the most common format for charity tournaments, company days and club fundraisers, and it's the friendliest way for golfers of very different skill levels to play together. This guide explains how a scramble works, why organizers like it, and what to expect when you show up.

## The basic idea

A scramble is a team format, usually played in foursomes. On every shot, each player on the team hits. The team then picks the best of those shots, and everyone plays their next shot from that spot. You repeat that until the ball is in the hole, and the team records one score for the hole.

Here's a single hole, step by step:

1. All four players hit a tee shot.
2. The team walks or drives to each ball and decides which one is in the best position. Say it's the drive in the middle of the fairway, 140 yards out.
3. The other three players pick up their balls and drop or place them near that spot. All four hit their second shots from there.
4. The team picks the best second shot, perhaps one that finished 12 feet from the hole, and all four putt from that spot.
5. If one of them makes the putt, the team scores a birdie. If nobody does, everyone putts again from wherever the best putt finished.

At the end of 18 holes, the team has one score, and the lowest team score wins.

## Why outings use it

Organizers choose a scramble for a few practical reasons.

**It keeps the pace moving.** A full field at an outing can be 120 to 144 players, often starting at the same time with a shotgun start. Because the team plays only one ball into the hole, nobody is grinding out a triple bogey, and the whole field tends to finish in four and a half to five hours. If you haven't played a shotgun before, our guide on [how shotgun starts work](/guides/how-shotgun-starts-work) walks through it.

**Everyone contributes.** A newer golfer might hit one great chip all day, and that chip might be the one the team uses. A strong player still has a lot to do, but nobody is left out.

**Scores are low and fun.** Good scramble teams often shoot well under par. Making a run of birdies is part of what makes the day enjoyable, even for people who rarely break 100 on their own.

**There's less pressure.** If you hit a bad shot, your teammates likely have a better one. That makes a scramble a good choice for people who play only a few times a year, or who are bringing a coworker or friend along for the first time.

## Common rules you'll see

Scrambles aren't governed by one official rulebook, so each outing sets its own. You'll usually get a rules sheet at check-in or a short announcement before the start. Common variations include:

- **Minimum drives.** Many outings require the team to use at least two, three or four tee shots from each player over the round. This keeps one long hitter from carrying the team on every hole.
- **Placement distance.** When you move your ball to the chosen spot, you typically place it within one club length, no closer to the hole. In a bunker or the rough, you usually stay in the same kind of lie. On the green, you place it within a few inches of the marked spot.
- **Forward tees.** Some outings let seniors, juniors or women play from forward tees. Check the rules sheet.
- **Gimmes.** Some outings count putts inside a certain length as made, often a putter's grip length, to keep the field moving.
- **Mulligans and extras.** Many charity outings sell mulligans (a redo of a shot) or a "string" you can use to move the ball. These raise money for the cause and lower scores a bit, and the rules sheet will say how many each player can use.

## Variations on the scramble

You'll also see a few related formats on outing listings:

- **Texas scramble.** Usually the same as a standard scramble, with a minimum-drives rule. The name means different things in different places, so read the rules.
- **Florida scramble (also called a step-aside scramble).** After choosing the best shot, the player whose shot was picked sits out the next shot. This slows the team down a little and makes it more of a challenge.
- **Two-person scramble.** The same idea with pairs instead of foursomes. Club member-guest events sometimes use this.
- **Shamble.** Everyone tees off, the team picks the best drive, and then each player plays their own ball into the hole from there. It mixes a scramble start with individual play.

If you want to see how those compare with best ball, read [scramble, best ball and shamble compared](/guides/scramble-best-ball-shamble).

## Handicaps in a scramble

Many outings don't use handicaps at all; the lowest gross team score wins. Others ask for each player's handicap and apply a team handicap, often a fraction of the combined total, so that a team of casual golfers has a chance against a team of strong players. Some outings flight the field, grouping teams by score after the round. You usually don't need an official handicap to enter. ## What to expect on the day

A typical scramble outing runs something like this, though timing varies:

- **Check-in and range.** Arrive 60 to 90 minutes before the start. You'll get a cart assignment, a rules sheet and sometimes a gift bag. Many courses open the driving range and practice green.
- **Lunch or breakfast.** Some outings serve a boxed lunch or a buffet before the round.
- **The start.** Most outings use a shotgun start, so every group begins on a different hole at the same time.
- **Contests on the course.** Closest to the pin and longest drive are common. These are usually individual contests within the scramble.
- **After the round.** Scores are posted, prizes are handed out, and there's often a dinner, raffle or auction.

## Is a scramble right for you?

If you're a beginner, a scramble is the most relaxed way to play competitive golf. You don't need to keep your own score, and you won't hold anyone up if you pick up after a few tough shots. If you're a strong player, a scramble is a chance to go low with a team and help a good cause.

Price doesn't tell you the format. A weekday scramble at a municipal course might be $100 to $175 per player, and a scramble at a private club might be $250 to $500 or more, but prices vary a lot by region and course. The game is the same either way.
