Charity golf outings are one of the most common ways local organizations raise money. Youth sports leagues, school booster clubs, hospitals, veterans groups, food banks, Rotary and Lions clubs, and memorial funds all run them. Most are open to anyone who pays the entry fee, whether you know the organizers or not. This guide covers who runs these events, how the money moves, and what the day usually looks like.

## Who runs a charity outing

Most outings are organized by a small committee of volunteers working with the course's events staff. The committee handles the fundraising side: finding sponsors, selling foursomes, collecting raffle prizes and promoting the event. The course handles the golf side: tee setup, carts, food and beverage, scoring and often the registration desk on the day.

Some larger nonprofits have a staff events coordinator who runs the outing every year, sometimes with an event-management company. Smaller groups may be running their first outing ever. Either way, the course is usually paid a set amount per player, and everything the organizers raise above their costs goes to the cause.

Outings take place at every kind of course. A youth hockey association might book its local municipal course on a Monday, while a hospital foundation might rent out a private club. Both can be good days of golf, and both can raise real money. If you want to plan one yourself, see [organizing a charity golf outing](/guides/organizing-a-charity-golf-outing).

## Where the money comes from

A charity outing usually raises money in several ways at once.

- **Entry fees.** Players pay per person or per foursome. Part of each fee covers the course's charge for golf, cart and food, and the rest is the margin that goes to the cause.
- **Sponsorships.** Local businesses pay to sponsor a hole, a cart, lunch, the beverage cart or the whole event. Sponsorships are often the biggest single source of money, because they have little direct cost. A hole sign might be a few hundred dollars, and a title sponsorship might run into the thousands, depending on the size of the event.
- **On-course extras.** Mulligans, raffle tickets, "beat the pro" holes and hole-in-one contests add money during the day.
- **Auctions and raffles.** Donated prizes, like a weekend getaway, signed memorabilia or a round at another course, are auctioned or raffled at the dinner.
- **Straight donations.** Some people who can't play give money anyway.

## Where the money goes

The organizers' first job is to cover costs. The biggest cost is what the course charges per player, which usually bundles green fees, cart, range balls and food. Other costs include prizes, signs, printed materials, gift bags and sometimes insurance for a hole-in-one prize.

What's left is the net, and that's what goes to the cause. How much of each dollar ends up as net varies a lot. An outing with strong sponsorship and donated prizes can keep a large share; an outing that pays for everything and has few sponsors will keep less. A well-run outing will often tell you what it raised last year and what the money paid for, such as scholarships, equipment or a specific program.

If you care about where the money goes, it's fair to ask the organizer. Their own event page often says who benefits, and the charity's annual report or IRS filings can tell you more.

## Charity, fundraiser or something else

Not every outing labeled "charity" is run by a registered charity. Some raise money for a family going through a hard time, a team trip or a club's own expenses. Those can be good causes, but your payment may be treated differently for tax purposes.

On Golf Outing Finder, a listing shows a "Charity" label when we've checked the organizer against IRS records. If we couldn't confirm it, the listing says "Fundraiser, charity status unverified." That label isn't a judgment about the cause; it just means we couldn't match the organizer to a registered charity. ## What the day usually looks like

Every outing is a little different, but most follow a similar schedule. Here's an example for an outing with a noon shotgun:

- **10:30 a.m. Check-in opens.** You give your name at the registration table, pick up a gift bag if there is one, and find out your starting hole and cart number. This is also where you can buy mulligans or raffle tickets.
- **10:45 a.m. Lunch and range.** Many outings serve a boxed lunch or a grill line. The driving range and practice green are usually open.
- **11:45 a.m. Welcome and rules.** An organizer thanks the sponsors, explains the format and any contests, and sends everyone to their carts.
- **12:00 p.m. Shotgun start.** Every group starts at once, each on a different hole.
- **On the course.** You'll pass sponsor signs at each tee and often volunteers running contests on certain holes. A beverage cart usually makes the rounds.
- **About 4:30 p.m. Round ends.** Teams turn in scorecards.
- **5:00 p.m. Dinner, awards and raffle.** Winners are announced, prizes are handed out, and the organizers share what the day raised.

Morning outings follow the same pattern a few hours earlier, often with breakfast instead of lunch. Most charity outings use a scramble format, which keeps things friendly for mixed skill levels. If that's new to you, see [what is a golf scramble](/guides/what-is-a-golf-scramble).

## What it costs to play

Entry fees cover a wide range. A weekday charity scramble at a municipal course might be $100 to $175 per player. Many outings at public and semi-private courses fall between $150 and $300. Outings at private clubs and resorts often run $250 to $600 or more per player. These are illustrative ranges; prices vary by region, course and season, and many outings offer a foursome price that's a little lower per person.

What you get for the fee also varies. Most include golf, a cart and at least one meal. Some include range balls, drinks on the course, a gift bag and dinner. Each listing on our site shows what's included, so you can compare outings side by side.

## Who can play

Almost anyone. You don't need to belong to the organization, live in the town or know anyone involved. Many outings welcome singles and will pair you with others. You also don't usually need a handicap.
