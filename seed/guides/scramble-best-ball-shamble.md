Golf outing listings usually name a format: scramble, best ball, shamble or something close to one of those. They're all team games, but they play very differently. The format affects how long the round takes, how much each player's game matters and how relaxed the day feels. Here's how the three most common formats work and how they compare.

## Scramble

In a scramble, every player on the team hits from the same spot each time. After everyone tees off, the team chooses the best drive. All players then hit their next shot from there, the team chooses the best of those, and so on until the ball is in the hole. The team records one score per hole.

**What it's like:** Fast, social and low-pressure. Only one ball is in play for the team after each round of shots, so nobody has to finish a bad hole. Scores are low, with strong teams often well under par.

**Who it suits:** Mixed groups, newer golfers, people who play a few times a year and anyone who wants a relaxed day. That's why it's the default for most charity and corporate outings.

**Common add-ons:** Minimum drives per player, mulligans for sale and a team handicap.

For a full walk-through, see [what is a golf scramble](/guides/what-is-a-golf-scramble).

## Best ball

In best ball, each player plays their own ball from tee to green, start to finish, the way they would on a normal round. On each hole, the team counts the lowest individual score. If your four scores on a hole are 4, 5, 5 and 6, the team score is 4.

Some outings count more than one score, such as "two best balls of four," where the two lowest scores on each hole are added together. Some vary it by hole: one ball counts on the first six holes, two on the next six and three on the last six.

**What it's like:** Closer to a regular round. Everyone plays every shot unless they're out of the hole, so it takes longer than a scramble. Your own game matters more, but a bad hole still won't hurt the team if a teammate plays it well.

**Who it suits:** Groups where most players are comfortable playing their own ball. It's common at club events, member-guest outings and two-person team tournaments.

**Handicaps:** Best ball often uses net scores, so each player's handicap strokes are applied hole by hole. That makes it fair for a mix of skill levels, but it does mean the organizer will want a handicap or an estimate.

**Pace tip:** If a teammate has already made a good score on a hole and you can't beat it, pick up. That keeps the field moving, which matters with a shotgun start.

## Shamble

A shamble mixes the two. Everyone tees off, the team picks the best drive, and all players move their ball to that spot. From there, each player plays their own ball into the hole. The team then counts one or more of the best individual scores, as in best ball.

**What it's like:** A middle ground. The shared drive takes the pressure off the tee shot, which is where many casual golfers struggle most, while everyone still plays their own approach shots, chips and putts.

**Who it suits:** Groups that want more of a real golf feel than a scramble but a quicker pace than best ball. It's a good fit for teams with one or two strong drivers.

**Variations:** Some shambles count one best score per hole; others count two. Some require a minimum number of drives from each player, as in a scramble.

## How they compare

**Pace.** Scramble is usually the fastest, shamble is in the middle and best ball is the slowest. With a full field on a shotgun start, a scramble might take four and a half hours and a best ball five or more, though course and field size make a big difference.

**Pressure on your own game.** Lowest in a scramble, moderate in a shamble, highest in best ball. In a scramble, your bad shots are rarely used. In best ball, your score counts whenever it's the lowest on the team.

**Scoring.** Scramble scores are the lowest. Shamble and best ball scores are higher and depend heavily on how many scores the team counts per hole.

**Handicaps.** Many scrambles skip handicaps or use a simple team adjustment. Best ball and shamble events are more likely to use individual handicaps. Most outings don't require an official handicap, but you may be asked for your typical score. See [do you need a handicap](/guides/do-you-need-a-handicap) for more.

**Beginner-friendliness.** Scramble is the easiest for a first-timer. A shamble is a fine second step. Best ball is best for people who already play regularly.

## Other formats you might see

- **Florida or step-aside scramble.** The player whose shot was chosen sits out the next shot.
- **Texas scramble.** Usually a scramble with a minimum-drives rule, though the name varies by region.
- **Modified scramble.** Often means a scramble to the green, with individual putting. Read the rules sheet.
- **Alternate shot.** Two players share one ball and take turns hitting it. It's less common at outings.
- **Stroke play or open tournament.** Individual play with each player's own score. You'll see this at open tournaments more than at charity outings.

Since there's no single official version of most outing formats, the rules sheet you get at check-in has the final word.

## Which format should you pick?

- **First outing, or a group with mixed experience:** a scramble.
- **Group of regular golfers who want to play their own ball:** best ball.
- **Somewhere in between:** a shamble.
- **Playing alone:** a scramble makes it easiest to fit in with a team you haven't met.

Price doesn't depend on format. You'll find scrambles at municipal courses for $100 to $175 per player and best ball events at private clubs for $300 or more, along with everything in between, depending on region and course.
