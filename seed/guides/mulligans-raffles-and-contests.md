Most golf outings are about more than the 18 holes. Between check-in and the awards dinner there's usually a table selling mulligans, a raffle with a prize board, and a few contests spread across the course. They're a big part of why outings feel different from a regular round, and for charity events they're often where a good share of the money gets raised.

This guide walks through the extras you're most likely to run into, how they work, and what they tend to cost. Details vary from event to event, so treat the numbers here as examples, not rules.

## Mulligans

A mulligan is a do-over. You hit a bad shot, use a mulligan, and hit again as if the first one never happened. In a regular round they're frowned on. At an outing, they're sold on purpose.

Here's how it usually goes:

- **They're sold at check-in.** You might see a price per mulligan, or a package such as two or three per player. A few dollars to $10 each is a common range, and packages often come with a small discount.
- **There's usually a limit.** Many events cap mulligans per player or per team so nobody buys their way to the trophy. Some say they can't be used on the green, or on a contest hole.
- **They're tracked loosely.** You might get tickets, wristbands or a mark on your scorecard. Hand one over or cross it off when you use it.

In a scramble, where your team picks the best shot and everyone plays from there, a mulligan gives the team another ball to choose from. If you're new to that format, our guide to [what a golf scramble is](/guides/what-is-a-golf-scramble) explains how it works.

Some outings fold mulligans into the entry fee. If the listing or the organizer's page says contests or extras are included, check whether that covers mulligans or only the on-course contests.

## On-course contests

Contests are usually set up on specific holes and marked with a sign on the tee. Most are free to enter as part of your round, though some events charge a small fee or sell entries.

### Closest to the pin

On a par 3, whoever lands their tee shot closest to the hole wins. There's often a marker or flag on the green where the leader writes their name, and the next group that gets closer replaces it. Some events hold separate contests for men and women, or put one on every par 3.

### Longest drive

On a chosen hole, usually a long par 4 or par 5, the longest drive that finishes in the fairway wins. A drive that ends up in the rough or the trees doesn't count, no matter how far it went. As with closest to the pin, the current leader's name goes on a marker.

### Straightest drive

A less common cousin of longest drive. A line is painted or roped down the middle of the fairway, and the ball that finishes nearest the line wins. It gives players who don't hit it far a real chance.

### Hole-in-one prizes

This is the contest with the big prize on the sign: a car, a cash prize, a golf trip, a set of clubs. Organizers rarely pay for the prize themselves. They buy hole-in-one insurance from a company that pays out if someone makes an ace on that hole.

Because there's a policy behind it, there are conditions. Common ones include:

- the shot has to be from a set yardage, often marked on the tee
- one or more witnesses who aren't in your group, sometimes a volunteer stationed at the hole
- you have to be an amateur, and sometimes an adult
- only your first tee shot counts, so a mulligan won't win the car

If you do make one, don't pick the ball out and start celebrating before you find the organizer. Read the rules on the sign before you hit, and ask a volunteer if anything is unclear.

### Putting contests

Many outings set up a putting contest on the practice green before the shotgun start. You might pay a few dollars for a try at a long putt, or play a short course of putts for a prize. It's a nice way to warm up and meet people while everyone gathers.

## Raffles, 50/50s and auctions

Raffles are where many charity outings raise a real share of their money. You buy tickets, often with a better deal for a bigger bundle, and winners are drawn during lunch or the awards.

A **50/50** is a simple raffle where half the pot goes to the winning ticket and half goes to the cause. The more tickets sold, the bigger both halves get.

Some events also hold silent auctions for items donated by local businesses.

### Raffle rules vary by state

Raffles count as a form of gambling in most places, and the rules differ a lot from state to state, and sometimes city to city. Some states allow raffles only for registered nonprofits, some require a permit or license, some limit prize values, and some restrict 50/50s or online ticket sales. If you're organizing an outing, check your state's rules before you print tickets. Our guide to [organizing a charity golf outing](/guides/organizing-a-charity-golf-outing) covers more of the planning side.

For players, the practical points are simpler. Bring some cash or a card, since tables may take one or the other. Ask whether you need to be present to win. And keep in mind that a large prize may count as taxable income, so the organizer may ask for your details if you win something big.

## Are these extras tax deductible?

Usually not in full, and often not at all. When you buy a raffle ticket or a mulligan, you're getting something in return, even if it's just a chance at a prize. That generally doesn't count as a charitable gift the way a straight donation does. Our guide on whether [charity golf entry fees are tax deductible](/guides/are-charity-golf-entry-fees-tax-deductible) explains the general idea. It's not tax advice, so check with a tax professional for your own situation.

## How to tell what an outing includes

On Golf Outing Finder, each listing shows what's included when the organizer's page says so, such as lunch, a cart, a gift or contests. If you see "contests" listed, the on-course contests are part of your entry. Mulligans and raffle tickets are usually sold separately.

The listing also shows the date, the shotgun time, the course type and the price per player and per foursome, so you can compare events before you click through. Registration happens on the organizer's own page, and that's where you'll find the full details on contests and prizes.

## A simple budget for the day

Set aside a little for extras beyond your entry fee, and spend what feels right. The round is just as fun if you skip the raffle table.
