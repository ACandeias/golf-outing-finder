If you own a business in a town with a golf course, sooner or later someone will ask you to sponsor a hole. It's one of the most common ways local businesses support a charity outing, and it's often the entry point to a sponsorship ladder that goes all the way up to having your name on the event itself.

This guide covers what a hole sponsorship is, the usual levels, what businesses tend to get, and the questions worth asking before you write a check. Whether you're a sponsor or an organizer putting together a sponsor packet, the basics are the same.

## What a hole sponsorship is

At its simplest, a hole sponsorship buys a sign with your business name or logo, placed on one tee box for the day. Every group that plays the hole sees it. Because most outings use a shotgun start, where every team starts at once on a different hole, every group passes every tee, so a hole sign gets seen by the whole field.

For the organizer, hole sponsorships matter because they usually cover costs. Entry fees often go mostly to the course for green fees, carts and food. Sponsorships are one of the main ways the event raises money beyond that.

## Common sponsor levels

Every outing builds its own packet, but most follow a similar pattern from smallest to largest. The prices below are illustrative ranges. Actual amounts vary a lot by region, the size of the event and the course.

### Hole or tee sign sponsor

The entry level. You get a sign on one tee, and often a mention in the program or on the event's website. Small community outings might ask somewhere around $100 to $300. Larger fundraisers at private clubs or resorts often ask more.

### Contest sponsor

Your name goes on a contest hole, such as closest to the pin, longest drive or the hole-in-one hole. These signs get extra attention because players stop to read the rules. Our guide to [mulligans, raffles and contests](/guides/mulligans-raffles-and-contests) explains how those contests work.

### Food, beverage and cart sponsors

Breakfast, lunch, dinner, the beverage cart, the golf carts and the driving range can each be sponsored. Your name appears on signs at the meal, on the cart or at the range. These often cost more than a single hole because more people see them, and for longer.

### Foursome packages

A step up that includes a team entry in the outing along with a sign. For a business, this combines advertising with a day out for clients or staff. Some outings only sell foursomes as part of a sponsorship. On Golf Outing Finder those listings show "Foursomes through sponsorship" instead of a single-player price.

### Presenting or title sponsor

The top level. Your name goes on the event itself, on banners, on the registration page and in announcements during the day. There's usually just one, and it often includes multiple foursomes, speaking time at the dinner and first right of refusal for the next year.

## What businesses actually get

Beyond the sign, a sponsorship usually comes with some mix of the following:

- your name or logo in the printed program and on the event's web page
- a thank-you during the awards or dinner
- mentions in the organizer's emails or social posts
- the option to staff your hole with a table, samples or a giveaway
- an item in the gift bag each golfer takes home
- a team entry at higher levels

The value is partly advertising and partly goodwill. A tee sign won't usually bring in customers the next morning, but it shows people in your community that you support a cause they care about. Businesses that send a staff member to stand at their hole and say hello often get the most out of it.

## Questions to ask before you sponsor

A short list worth running through with the organizer:

1. **What's the deadline for artwork?** Signs are often printed a few weeks before the event. Miss it and your sign may say your name in plain text, or not appear at all.
2. **What size and format do they need?** Ask for dimensions and the file type they prefer.
3. **Which hole do you get?** Some organizers let sponsors pick, often in the order they signed up. A hole near the clubhouse or a par 3 where groups wait can get more attention.
4. **Can you staff the hole?** Ask whether you're allowed to set up a table, and whether food or drinks are allowed there.
5. **Do you keep the sign?** Some organizers give signs back after the event, which is handy if you sponsor every year.
6. **Is the organizer a registered charity?** If that matters to you, ask for the organization's name as registered and its tax ID.
7. **What part is tax deductible?** If you get something of value in return, such as a foursome, only part of the payment may count as a charitable gift. Our guide to whether [charity golf entry fees are tax deductible](/guides/are-charity-golf-entry-fees-tax-deductible) covers the general idea. Check with your own tax professional for advice.

## For organizers building a sponsor packet

If you're on the other side, keep the packet short and specific. List each level, its price, exactly what it includes and the deadline. Put the same information on your public event page so sponsors can share it. Local businesses get many requests, so a clear ask with a plain price and a simple way to pay goes a long way.
